# Markov Script: Fixture Housing-and-Rates Video

> Demonstration fixture. Names, claims, IDs, and URLs are illustrative.

## Production verdict

**Viable with qualifications.** The historical rate claim is supported, but the
requested housing forecast must be presented as conditional.

## Recommended thesis

A measured rate increase is real and relevant, but it is not a complete housing
forecast: the evidence supports a multi-factor explanation, not certainty.

## Audience promise

Give a general audience a clear explanation of what the historical record shows,
why the forecast is less certain, and which conditions common coverage leaves out.

## Recommended angle

Lead with the difference between measuring a rate change and predicting a housing
market. The angle is evidence-led and useful because it shows exactly where a
clean number ends and a contested causal story begins. The risk is implying that
uncertainty means rates do not matter; the script explicitly avoids that move.

## Title options

- The Rate Hike Was Real. The Housing Forecast Is Harder.
- What Interest Rates Can—and Cannot—Tell Us About Home Prices
- The Missing Variables in a One-Line Housing Prediction

## Hook options

- One number in this housing forecast is easy to verify. The conclusion built on
  top of it is not.
- A two-point rate increase sounds like a complete explanation—until you inspect
  what the housing evidence actually measures.
- The strongest part of this prediction is historical. The weakest part is the
  future it claims to settle.

## Complete spoken narration

One number in this housing forecast is easy to verify. The conclusion built on
top of it is not.

The original video says the policy rate increased by two percentage points. At
one minute and one second, that statement is presented as a settled fact [C1].
And in the period the presenter identifies, the official series does move from
three percent to five percent [E1]. On that narrow historical point, the source
and the independent record line up.

But a measured rate change is not the same thing as a complete housing forecast.
The video moves from the historical increase to a prediction: national housing
prices will fall the following year [C2]. That may be a reasonable scenario, but
it is still a prediction. Its assumptions and time horizon have to remain visible.

The strongest inspected housing passage explains why. It identifies interest
rates as one contributor, then places them beside supply, income, migration, and
credit availability [E2]. In other words, the rate matters, but its effect travels
through a market with several moving parts.

Supply is one useful way to see the complication. If demand weakens where homes
are plentiful, prices may respond differently than they do where construction is
constrained. Income and migration can also change who is competing for the
available stock. Lending standards can determine whether the policy-rate move
reaches borrowers in the way a simple headline assumes. Those mechanisms do not
disprove the forecast. They qualify it.

That distinction changes the responsible narration. We can say the historical
rate increased by two points [C1]. We can say the presenter predicts a national
price decline [C2]. We can also say that the inspected research does not make
that decline automatic. What we should not say is that one verified input proves
one future outcome.

There is another unresolved question: what does “housing prices” mean here?
Nominal national prices, inflation-adjusted prices, transaction volume, and
regional prices can tell different stories. Without a defined measure, a future
claim can appear correct or incorrect depending on which series is selected
after the fact.

So the useful conclusion is neither endorsement nor debunking. The historical
number survives inspection. The forecast survives only as a qualified scenario.
The next step is to define the outcome, compare similar periods and regions, and
test the non-rate factors rather than hiding them.

That gives us a stronger story than the original shortcut. We know what changed.
We know where the evidence is direct. And we know exactly where prediction begins.

## Fact-check appendix

### C1 — Supported

The policy rate increased by two percentage points during the measured period.

- E1 supports the exact numerical change using an official historical series.
- Scope limit: C1 does not establish the housing effect.

### C2 — Qualified prediction

National housing prices will fall the following year.

- E2 qualifies the prediction with additional mechanisms and conditions.
- The national measure and one-year horizon lack direct confirming evidence.

## Do not repeat

- “The rate increase guarantees a national housing decline.”
- “The evidence proves prices will fall next year.”
- “Other housing-market variables are negligible.”
- Any unqualified version of C2 stated as a current fact.
