# Product Story Design

A landing page is not a decorated list of features. It is a sequence that helps a visitor recognize their situation, understand the promised progress, believe the proof, and take the next action.

## Define what is being sold

Before layout or copy, write four statements:

1. **Functional progress** — what work becomes easier or newly possible?
2. **Emotional progress** — what frustration, uncertainty, or fear disappears?
3. **Social progress** — how does the user want to be perceived by others?
4. **Dream** — what better future does repeated use create?

Then identify the triggering moment in the user's own language. The page should begin near that moment, not with the product category.

## Build the story from the product's real behavior

A strong product page usually needs these beats, but not necessarily in this visual order:

- **Recognition:** the visitor sees the situation that brought them here.
- **Core action:** the page shows what the user gives the product.
- **Transformation:** one realistic input becomes one useful output.
- **Difference:** the page demonstrates what happens here that does not happen in the default substitute.
- **Proof:** real examples, working interactions, source material, metrics, or transparent limitations.
- **Outcomes:** the finished jobs the user can accomplish.
- **Next action:** a truthful CTA that names what will happen.

Do not use these beats as a fixed template. Compose them according to the product and the strongest available proof.

## Hero requirements

The first screen must answer within a few seconds:

- What can I bring here?
- What will happen to it?
- What useful thing will I leave with?
- What can I do next?

Prefer showing the core interaction or a real transformation. Avoid leading with:

- Category jargon.
- “AI-powered” as the primary benefit.
- A fake dashboard.
- Abstract claims that could describe any competitor.
- A screenshot inside a laptop frame when the screenshot does not explain the job.

The primary CTA must be honest. “Open” must open a working product. “Try” must permit a trial. Installation instructions should be labeled as installation.

## Show one complete transformation

One credible before-and-after is more persuasive than many shallow feature blocks.

Show:

- The actual starting material.
- The intermediate insight only when it clarifies the difference.
- The finished result.
- The specific part of the result that proves the promise.

Use real or clearly labeled sample content. Never fabricate customer quotes, logos, evidence, or usage metrics.

## Write outcome-first copy

Use the customer's verbs and nouns.

Prefer:

- “Paste a video.”
- “Find the missing context.”
- “Follow this connection.”
- “Turn it into a script.”

Avoid:

- “Unlock.”
- “Supercharge.”
- “Revolutionize.”
- “Harness the power of.”
- “Seamlessly transform.”

Headings should make a promise, state a useful contrast, or reveal something specific. A clever heading that hides the meaning has failed.

## Design the page around proof density

Not every section deserves equal visual weight.

- Give the core transformation the largest canvas.
- Let supporting features remain subordinate.
- Use repeated cards only when comparison is genuinely the task.
- Use labels, diagrams, screenshots, media, and annotations because they explain—not because the page needs decoration.
- Remove sections that repeat the same promise with different wording.

## Distinguish the product from substitutes

Name the current workaround and show what disappears.

Examples of useful contrasts:

- Many tools and manual transfers become one continuous workflow.
- A summary becomes a new connection or finished output.
- An untraceable answer becomes inspectable evidence.
- A pile of inputs becomes a decision or artifact.

Do not attack competitors vaguely. Demonstrate the difference through the product sequence.

## Acceptance test

Remove the logo and product name. Then ask:

- Is the product still identifiable from the interaction and content?
- Does the page sell a specific form of progress?
- Is the dream visible without becoming a manifesto?
- Is the proof real enough to support the promise?
- Does the page end with a clear next action?

If the page could sell an unrelated SaaS product after swapping nouns, rebuild the story.
